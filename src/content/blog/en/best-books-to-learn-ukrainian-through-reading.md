# Best Books to Learn Ukrainian Through Reading: From Beginner to Advanced

Ukrainian is one of the most rewarding languages you can pick up by reading -- and one of the more demanding ones to start. Its literature stretches from village comedies that children memorise to poetry that helped invent the modern language, the writing system is genuinely learnable in a couple of weeks, and a single well-chosen page can teach you more living grammar than an hour of drills. But there is no honest way to dress this up: Ukrainian is hard for English speakers. The Foreign Service Institute places it in Category IV, the same tier as Russian, Finnish, Hungarian, Turkish, and Thai, estimating roughly 1,100 class hours to reach professional working proficiency. That is not the very hardest tier the FSI defines, but it is close, and the reason is grammar and vocabulary, not the alphabet.

Reading is one of the best tools you have for closing that gap. The hard parts of Ukrainian -- the seven-case noun system, the perfective and imperfective verb pairs, the way word order shifts to carry emphasis -- are exactly the things that drilling teaches you to recognise but not to feel. Reading teaches you to feel them, because you meet the same case ending and the same aspect pair hundreds of times in contexts that make their meaning obvious. You do not memorise that the genitive is used after numbers; you simply stop being surprised by it.

There is one wrinkle specific to Ukrainian, and it matters for everything that follows: graded-reader material is genuinely scarcer than it is for the big Western languages. You will not find shelves of carefully levelled A1 chapter books the way you would for French or Spanish. A handful of good bilingual beginner readers exist, and after that you are mostly choosing between children's books and real literature. That scarcity is precisely why parallel text -- the same passage in Ukrainian and English, sentence by sentence -- matters more here than almost anywhere else. It lets you read material that would otherwise be a level or two above you, because the translation is never more than a glance away.

Interest in learning Ukrainian has risen sharply in recent years. Duolingo reported a 485% surge in new Ukrainian learners in early 2022, and over the following year more than 1.3 million people started the course -- a wave driven largely by younger learners and by people in neighbouring countries hosting Ukrainian speakers. If you are one of them, this guide is for you. Below are real, verifiable Ukrainian books organised by CEFR level, with an honest note on each: why it works, and what to watch for.

## The Alphabet Comes First

Before any book, you need the alphabet. There is no shortcut, but the good news is that it is short and consistent.

### The Ukrainian Cyrillic Variant

Ukrainian uses a Cyrillic alphabet, but not the same one as Russian -- a distinction worth getting straight early, because a lot of online material conflates the two. Ukrainian has letters Russian does not: **і** (a plain "ee" sound), **ї** (a "yi" glide), **є** (a "ye" sound, the mirror image of Russian's э), and **ґ** (a hard "g", distinct from г, which in Ukrainian is a soft "h"-like sound). It also lacks several letters that Russian has -- there is no ы, no ъ, and no э. If you have studied Russian, do not assume the values carry over; и in Ukrainian sounds roughly like the Russian ы, and the letter г is one of the clearest audible markers that you are hearing Ukrainian and not Russian.

The whole system is broadly phonetic. Once you know the letters, you can pronounce almost any word you see, which is a real gift compared with the spelling chaos of English or French. Spend a week or two with the alphabet until you can sound out words without consciously decoding each letter. Until reading is automatic, every sentence costs too much effort to enjoy, and enjoyment is what keeps you reading.

A useful trick: start with cognates and international words, which are everywhere. **Кафе** (cafe), **таксі** (taxi), **музика** (music), **студент** (student), **Україна** (Ukraine). Reading words you already know, just in a new script, builds decoding speed without the burden of new vocabulary at the same time.

### Choosing Your First Ukrainian Book

Once the alphabet is automatic, the single most common mistake is reaching for real literature too early. A novel you cannot follow does not teach you Ukrainian; it teaches you that Ukrainian is miserable, and then you quit. The right first book is one where you understand most of what you read and look things up rarely -- comprehension with a little friction, not a wall.

For a Category IV language with thin graded-reader coverage, that almost always means one of three things: a purpose-built bilingual beginner reader, a children's book, or any text read with sentence-level parallel translation so the meaning is always within reach. The sections below are ordered so you can find the right entry point for where you actually are, not where you wish you were. Be honest about your level. If a sample page is a struggle, drop down. There is no prize for suffering through a book that is too hard, and there is a real penalty: you stop reading. ## Beginner Ukrainian Books (A1-A2)

At this level your goal is consolidation, not conquest. You want to see the most common few hundred words again and again, get comfortable with the present tense and basic cases, and build the simple habit of reading Ukrainian every day. Sentences should be short. Plots can be thin. That is fine.

### Purpose-Built Bilingual Readers

The most reliable starting point is a reader written specifically for learners, with Ukrainian and English on the same page. The clearest example is the **First Ukrainian Reader for Beginners** by Olena Dniprova, published as part of a "Graded Ukrainian Readers" series and aimed squarely at A1-A2. It runs a sequence of short, everyday stories -- meeting people, studying, looking for work -- with the English translation alongside, so you can check meaning instantly, and it comes with online audio tracks you can reach via QR codes in the book.

**Why it works:** It is one of the few resources built from the ground up for beginners, with vocabulary controlled to your level and translation always at hand. The everyday topics mean the words you learn are the words you will actually reuse.

**What to watch for:** Purpose-built readers are functional rather than literary -- the stories exist to carry the vocabulary, not to move you. Do not expect art. Expect a clean, low-stress on-ramp, and once you have outgrown it, move on without sentiment.

A comparable option is a collection like **55 Bilingual Short Stories to Learn Ukrainian for Beginner**, pitched at A1-A2, which pairs each short story with a vocabulary list, grammar notes, and comprehension questions. The story-plus-exercises format suits learners who like a little structure rather than pure free reading.

### Children's Picture Books and Early Readers

The other dependable beginner source is children's literature, which uses simple sentence structures and concrete, high-frequency vocabulary by design. Ukrainian has a deep tradition here. The catch is that most Ukrainian children's books are published for native Ukrainian children, so they will not come with an English translation -- which is where a parallel-translation reading app earns its place, letting you tap any sentence you cannot parse.

**Why it works:** Children's books were written to be understood by people who are still learning the language, which describes you exactly. Sentences are short, the present tense dominates, and the pictures carry meaning when the words do not.

**What to watch for:** Books aimed at very young children sometimes lean on baby-talk, rhyme, and invented words that will not serve you. Aim for books written for roughly ages six to ten -- old enough for real sentences, young enough that the language stays controlled.

### The Little Prince (Маленький принц)

Antoine de Saint-Exupéry's **Маленький принц** is the single most reliable bridge book in almost any language, and Ukrainian is no exception. It exists in official Ukrainian translation, including bilingual editions, and even appears in side-by-side parallel-text formats online. You very likely already know the story, which removes the single biggest obstacle to reading in a new language: not knowing what is going on.

**Why it works:** Knowing the plot in advance lets you spend all your effort on the language instead of the story. The prose is deceptively simple, the chapters are short, and a bilingual edition gives you the English line whenever you need it. Because the book is famous, a Ukrainian translation is easy to find.

**What to watch for:** "Simple" is relative. The book's gentle, philosophical tone uses some abstract vocabulary ("to tame", "what is essential is invisible to the eye") that is a notch harder than the everyday words in a graded reader. Treat it as the top of the A2 range -- a stretch goal for a confident beginner rather than a first book.

## Intermediate Ukrainian Books (B1-B2)

This is where reading starts to pay off in a big way, and also where many learners stall. You have the basics, but real books still feel like a wall of unfamiliar words. The fix is not to grind vocabulary lists -- it is to read material that is interesting enough to pull you through the discomfort, with parallel translation to keep the friction survivable. If you have felt stuck here, our piece on the [intermediate plateau](/en/blog/intermediate-plateau-how-to-break-through/) goes deeper.

### Vsevolod Nestaiko -- Тореадори з Васюківки

If there is one Ukrainian book to aim for as an early intermediate learner, this is a strong candidate. Vsevolod Nestaiko is Ukraine's best-loved children's author, and **Тореадори з Васюківки** ("Toreadors from Vasyukivka", first published in 1964) is his most famous work -- the funny, warm-hearted adventures of two village boys, Pavlusha and Yava, who dig a "subway" under a pigsty, stage a bullfight with a cow, and generally get into glorious trouble. It is a fixture of the Ukrainian school curriculum and was placed on the Hans Christian Andersen Honour List.

**Why it works:** This is the sweet spot for an upper-children's-literature read -- the language is real and idiomatic Ukrainian, but the world is concrete, the humour is broad, and the narrative momentum carries you forward. Because generations of Ukrainians grew up on it, it also gives you cultural common ground that pays off in conversation.

**What to watch for:** It is written for native-speaking children, so the vocabulary is wider than a graded reader and includes village life, childhood slang, and period detail. The comedy occasionally relies on wordplay that is hard to catch at first. This is a book to read with parallel text within reach, not bare.

### Andrey Kurkov -- with an honest caveat

Andrey Kurkov is probably the best-known Ukrainian novelist internationally, and his breakthrough novel **Death and the Penguin** (Смерть стороннього, 1996) -- a deadpan, surreal story of a lonely Kyiv writer who adopts a depressed penguin and drifts into writing obituaries for the mafia -- is one of the most accessible literary novels to come out of Ukraine: short chapters, clear prose, dark comedy.

Here is the caveat, and it matters: Kurkov writes his fiction in **Russian**, not Ukrainian. His novels reach Ukrainian readers in translation. So if your goal is specifically Ukrainian-language input, you would be reading a translated text, not the author's own words -- still perfectly good Ukrainian, but worth knowing. If you are learning both languages, or are flexible about which, Kurkov is an outstanding accessible-modern-fiction choice. If you want Ukrainian originals only, treat him as optional and lean on the authors below instead. (Readers focused on Russian may prefer our companion guide, [Best Books to Learn Russian Through Reading](/en/blog/best-books-to-learn-russian-through-reading/).)

**Why it works:** The Ukrainian translation reads cleanly, the chapters are short and self-contained, and the plot is genuinely gripping, which keeps you turning pages -- the single most important property of an intermediate reading book.

**What to watch for:** As above -- it is a translation, not an original. And the deadpan tone leans on irony and understatement, which can be subtle for a learner. The vocabulary is solidly B1-B2.

### Serhiy Zhadan -- the modern voice of the east

Serhiy Zhadan is one of the central figures of contemporary Ukrainian literature, a poet and novelist from the Luhansk region who writes about industrial eastern Ukraine in his own language. His novel **Ворошиловград** (Voroshilovgrad, 2010) won the BBC Ukrainian Book of the Year and later Book of the Decade; his later novel **Інтернат** (translated as "The Orphanage", 2017) follows a Ukrainian-language teacher crossing a war zone to bring his nephew home, and has been called the definitive novel of the war.

**Why it works:** Zhadan writes in living, contemporary Ukrainian -- the language as it is actually spoken and felt now, not a museum version. For a learner who wants to read serious modern fiction in the original, he is one of the most important authors to grow into.

**What to watch for:** Be honest about the difficulty. Zhadan's prose is rich, atmospheric, and full of regional colour and slang; *The Orphanage* in particular is bleak and demanding in subject matter. This is upper-B2 territory at the earliest, and it rewards being read once you are genuinely comfortable, with parallel text as a safety net. Start with him too early and you will bounce off.

## Advanced Ukrainian Books (C1+)

At this level you can mostly read for pleasure and pick up the rest from context. The job now is breadth, nuance, and the harder registers: poetry, classic prose, and the stylistically ambitious modern novel. Parallel text becomes a tool you reach for occasionally rather than constantly. If you find your reading has outpaced your listening at this stage, our note on [reading well but not understanding speech](/en/blog/can-read-but-cant-understand-speech/) explains why and what to do.

### Oksana Zabuzhko -- Польові дослідження з українського сексу

Oksana Zabuzhko is one of the most important living Ukrainian writers, and her 1996 novel **Польові дослідження з українського сексу** ("Fieldwork in Ukrainian Sex") was a landmark -- it topped the Ukrainian bestseller list through the 1990s and was later voted the book that most influenced Ukrainian society in the country's first fifteen years of independence. It is the most widely translated work of new Ukrainian prose.

**Why it works:** It is a central text of post-independence Ukrainian culture, and reading it in the original gives you access to a voice that defined a generation. Few books reward advanced effort more.

**What to watch for:** This is genuinely difficult, and not only because of the subject matter. The novel is written in long, serpentine, stream-of-consciousness sentences that slide into parenthetical asides and fragments of verse, with no conventional chapter breaks. It is hard for native readers and harder still for learners. Do not attempt it until your Ukrainian is solid; this is a C1+ book in every sense, and even then, keep a translation nearby.

### Taras Shevchenko -- Кобзар

You cannot understand Ukrainian literature -- or the Ukrainian language -- without **Кобзар** (Kobzar), the poetry collection of Taras Shevchenko (1814-1861). Shevchenko's verse is regarded as the foundation of modern Ukrainian literature and, to a real degree, of the modern Ukrainian literary language itself. The collection first appeared in 1840 and grew over his lifetime into the national book.

**Why it works:** Nothing else gives you the same access to the emotional and historical core of Ukrainian identity. Shevchenko wove together folk rhythms and the spoken vernacular, and his lines are quoted by ordinary Ukrainians to this day. Reading him is reading the source.

**What to watch for:** It is poetry, and old poetry at that -- by far the hardest entry on this list. The text is dotted with archaisms, folk forms, and condensed poetic syntax, and verse drops the helpful redundancy of prose that lets you guess your way through. Approach it line by line, with a parallel translation and ideally notes, and treat individual poems as deep study rather than extensive reading. This is a C1+ project, not a casual read.

### Lesya Ukrainka and Ivan Franko

Rounding out the classical canon are the two writers usually named alongside Shevchenko as the greatest in the language. **Lesya Ukrainka** (1871-1913), widely considered the finest female poet in Ukrainian, wrote drama, poetry, and prose drawing on the great themes of European culture -- early Christianity, ancient Troy, the Don Juan legend (her verse drama *Камінний господар*, "The Stone Host"). **Ivan Franko** (1856-1916) was astonishingly prolific across poetry, novels, plays, and criticism; his historical novel *Захар Беркут* (Zakhar Berkut) and the long poem *Мойсей* (Moses) are good places to start.

**Why it works:** Together with Shevchenko they form the backbone of the literary canon. Reading them gives you the references, the registers, and the historical depth that mark a genuinely advanced reader.

**What to watch for:** This is nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century literary language, with older vocabulary, longer periods, and stylistic conventions that have shifted since. Franko's prose is generally more approachable than Lesya Ukrainka's dense verse drama; if you are choosing where to begin, start with Franko's narrative prose and work up to Ukrainka's dramatic poetry. Both are firmly C1+.

## The Bottom Line

Ukrainian is hard, and it is worth being clear-eyed about that: an FSI Category IV language, a Cyrillic alphabet with its own letters (і, ї, є, ґ) that you must not confuse with Russian's, a seven-case grammar, and a thinner shelf of graded readers than learners of French or Spanish enjoy. None of that makes it unlearnable through reading. It just changes the order of operations.

Learn the alphabet first, properly, until reading is automatic. Then start lower than your ego wants -- a bilingual beginner reader like Dniprova's *First Ukrainian Reader*, or *The Little Prince* in a Ukrainian edition. Move up to children's classics like Nestaiko's *Тореадори з Васюківки* and accessible modern fiction (keeping in mind that Kurkov writes in Russian, so you would read him in translation). Grow into Zhadan's contemporary voice, and eventually into the canon -- Shevchenko's *Кобзар*, Lesya Ukrainka, Ivan Franko -- where parallel text and patience do the heavy lifting.

Because levelled material is scarce, the single most useful thing you can do is read with sentence-level parallel translation, so you are never stuck and never tempted to quit. Pick the book just slightly above your level, keep the English line one tap away, read a little every day, and let the language accumulate. That is how reading turns a Category IV language from a wall into a slope. Read every day, choose books you actually want to finish, and Ukrainian will open up faster than the hour-count suggests.
