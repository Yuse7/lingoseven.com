# How to Read Faster in a Foreign Language

You sit down with a novel in the language you are learning. You read the first sentence. Then you read it again, because the first pass did not stick. There is a word you almost know, so you tap it. Then another. By the time you reach the bottom of the page, twenty minutes have gone, you have looked up nine words, and you could not tell anyone what just happened in the story. In your own language, that same page would have taken ninety seconds and required no effort at all.

This is the quiet frustration of intermediate reading. You are no longer a beginner. You can decode the words, you mostly follow the grammar, and yet the whole thing moves at a crawl. It feels like wading through water. Worse, the slowness makes everything else harder: when reading is this effortful, you do not want to do much of it, and the small amount you do does not give you the volume you actually need to improve.

The good news is that slow reading is normal, predictable, and fixable. It is not a sign that you are bad at languages. Every fluent reader of a second language went through this stage, and the path out of it is well understood. The bad news, if you want to call it that, is that there is no trick. No app, no eye-movement exercise, and no "speed reading" course will let you skip the work. Reading speed in a foreign language is a byproduct of comprehension and volume, not a skill you train directly.

This article walks through why foreign-language reading is so slow, what actually makes it faster, and which popular shortcuts are a waste of your time. Most of it comes down to one idea: stop trying to read faster and start building the thing that makes fast reading possible.

## Why Foreign-Language Reading Is So Slow

To understand how to speed up, it helps to know what is eating your time. Slow second-language reading comes from a few separate problems stacked on top of each other.

**Decoding still costs effort.** In your native language, recognizing a word is instant and unconscious. You do not "read" the word *table*; you simply know it the moment your eyes land on it. In a foreign language, especially at the intermediate stage, word recognition is still partly conscious. Your brain spends real effort matching letters to sounds to meanings. Multiply that tiny cost across every word on the page and it adds up to a serious tax.

**Low automaticity.** This is the technical name for the decoding problem, and it is the heart of the matter. Reading researchers describe a shift from controlled processing (slow, effortful, conscious) to automatic processing (fast, effortless, unconscious). Native readers recognize almost every word automatically. Foreign-language readers recognize some words automatically, some with effort, and some not at all. The ones that need effort interrupt the flow, and every interruption costs you both time and comprehension.

**Constant lookups.** Each time you stop to check a word, you do more than lose a few seconds. You break the thread of the sentence. By the time you return from the dictionary, you often have to reread the clause you were in the middle of, which means you read it twice. Frequent lookups do not just slow you down arithmetically; they fragment the act of reading into disconnected pieces.

**Subvocalization and rereading.** Most readers "hear" words in their head as they read. In your native language this inner voice runs at full conversational speed and does not slow you down. In a foreign language, the inner voice is often slower, less confident, and prone to stumbling over unfamiliar pronunciations, which drags the whole process down. On top of that, when comprehension is shaky you reread sentences to make sure you got them, sometimes two or three times.

**Working memory overload.** A sentence in a foreign language asks more of your short-term memory. You hold the beginning of a long sentence while you parse the end, you track which pronoun refers to whom, and you juggle grammar you have not fully automated. When too much is happening at once, you lose the start of the sentence before you reach its end, and back you go to the beginning.

Notice that none of these are about how fast your eyes move. They are all about how much effort each word and each sentence demands. That is the key insight, and it points directly at the solution.

## What Actually Builds Speed: Automaticity Through Volume

If slow reading comes from effortful word recognition, then fast reading comes from making word recognition effortless. You do that by meeting words again and again until your brain stops working to recognize them. There is no shortcut for this. You build automaticity by reading a lot.

This is the case for [extensive reading](/en/blog/extensive-vs-intensive-reading/): reading large amounts of text that is easy enough to enjoy, for meaning and pleasure rather than study. The mechanism is simple. Every time you encounter a word in context, the connection between its form and its meaning gets a little stronger and a little faster. The first time you meet a word you might puzzle over it. The tenth time, you recognize it with mild effort. The fiftieth time, it is automatic, and it no longer slows you down at all. Speed is what automaticity feels like from the inside.

The research backs this up with concrete numbers. In one longitudinal study, a cohort of Japanese learners of English raised their reading speed by around 31 percent over roughly 30 weeks of extensive reading. That gain did not come from speed drills. It came from volume: from reading enough that more and more of the words on the page became instantly recognizable.

This is also why short, intense study sessions on hard texts do so little for your speed. If you spend an hour decoding two pages of a difficult novel, you have not given your brain many repetitions of common words at a comfortable pace. You have given it a small number of painful repetitions of rare words. The path to speed runs the other way: a large number of easy repetitions of common words. Reading is, in this sense, the single most effective thing you can do, which is one reason we keep coming back to the argument that [reading is the best way to learn a language](/en/blog/why-reading-is-the-best-way-to-learn-a-language/).

How much volume? More than most learners think. The vocabulary and exposure needed to read fluently runs into the millions of words of text over time. We go into the specific numbers in our piece on [how many words you need to read in a foreign language](/en/blog/how-many-words-to-read-in-a-foreign-language/), but the short version is that you should think in terms of books finished, not pages studied.

## Comprehension Comes First, and the 98 Percent Rule

Here is the part that feels backwards: to read faster, you need to read material that is easy. Not material at the edge of your ability, not material that "challenges" you on every line, but material you can mostly understand without help.

The reasoning comes from research on vocabulary coverage, which asks how much of the words in a text you need to already know in order to read it comfortably and learn from it without constant interruption. The widely cited benchmark is around 98 percent. That means roughly one unknown word in every fifty, or about one or two unknown words per typical page. At that level you can follow the story, guess most new words from context, and keep moving. Drop to 95 percent coverage (one unknown word in twenty) and reading becomes noticeably harder; the unknowns come often enough that you lose the thread and reach for the dictionary again and again.

This explains why your reading is slow when you push too hard. A text where you do not know one word in ten is not "challenging," it is the wrong level. Every few words you hit something unknown, every unknown costs you a lookup or a guess, and the lookups fragment the reading until there is no flow left to speak of. You cannot build automaticity on a text like that, because you never get the smooth repetition that automaticity requires.

Choosing the right level is therefore not a comfort preference, it is a speed strategy. Reading slightly below your perceived ability is what lets you read fast, read a lot, and improve. If you are unsure where to start, our guide to [the best books by language level](/en/blog/best-books-by-language-level/) can help you find texts that sit in that comfortable 98 percent zone rather than the frustrating one.

There is an honest tension here. Easy reading builds speed and volume; harder reading stretches your vocabulary and grammar. Both have a place, which is exactly the distinction between extensive and intensive reading. But if your specific problem is speed, the answer leans heavily toward easier texts in larger quantities.

## Reading While Listening: A Pacer for Your Eyes

One of the most effective ways to break the habit of slow, halting reading is to let someone else set the pace. When you read a text while listening to a native-speed audio recording of it at the same time, the audio pulls your eyes forward at a steady, natural rhythm. You cannot stall on a word for ten seconds, because the voice has already moved on, and you move with it.

This does several things at once. It trains your eyes to keep moving instead of fixating and rereading. It connects the written word to its real pronunciation, which strengthens recognition. And it gradually raises your comfortable reading speed toward the speed of natural speech, which is a reasonable target for fluent reading. The technique is old, well supported, and pleasant to do. We cover the details, including how to choose audio that matches your level, in our guide to the [reading-while-listening method](/en/blog/reading-while-listening-method/).

Reading while listening also helps a problem many learners do not realize they have: a slow or unreliable inner voice. If you can decode a sentence on paper but could not understand it spoken aloud, your written and spoken language are out of sync. That gap is common, and it is the subject of its own article on why you [can read but cannot understand speech](/en/blog/can-read-but-cant-understand-speech/). Pairing text with audio closes the gap from both sides, and a faster, more confident inner voice means faster silent reading later.

A word of caution: the audio should be at a level you can mostly follow. If you understand almost none of what you hear, the recording is not pacing your reading, it is just rushing past you. Match the difficulty the same way you would for the text alone.

## Reducing Lookups Without Losing Comprehension

Dictionary dependence is one of the biggest brakes on reading speed, and it is one of the easiest to loosen once you understand the trade-off. Every lookup buys you certainty about one word and costs you flow across the whole sentence. At the right reading level, that trade is usually a bad one.

The skill to develop is tolerating ambiguity: letting an unknown word go by when you can still follow the sentence without it. Most words you do not know are not essential to the meaning of the passage, and many of them you can infer well enough from context. Stopping for every one of them is what keeps you stuck. The aim is not to ban the dictionary, it is to use it for the words that actually block comprehension and skip it for the rest.

If you read at 98 percent coverage, this becomes natural, because there are only one or two unknown words per page in the first place. If you find yourself wanting to look up something on every line, that is usually a signal that the text is too hard, not that you lack discipline. We lay out the full approach, including when a lookup is worth it and when it is not, in our guide to [reading without a dictionary](/en/blog/how-to-read-without-a-dictionary/).

There is also a practical middle ground that modern tools make possible. The reason traditional lookups are so costly is the round trip: stop reading, switch to a dictionary, type the word, read the entry, find your place again. A tap-to-translate feature that shows the meaning instantly, in place, without leaving the page, removes most of that cost. You still get the meaning, but the flow barely breaks. That makes the occasional lookup cheap enough that it stops being a speed problem.

## Re-reading: The Underrated Speed Trick

Here is a method that sounds too simple to work: read the same passage more than once. Reading a text a second time is one of the most reliable ways to feel, immediately, what fluent reading is like.

The first pass through any passage is the slow one. You are meeting the vocabulary, parsing the grammar, and figuring out what is happening. On the second pass, all of that is already done. The words are familiar, the structure is known, and your eyes can move at something close to their natural speed. Re-reading lets you experience faster reading on text you have already paid the comprehension cost for, and that experience is itself training: it shows your reading system what the target pace feels like.

Repeated reading has a long track record in fluency research for exactly this reason. It is also pleasant, because the second pass of a good passage is usually more enjoyable than the first; you can appreciate the writing once you are no longer fighting to understand it. You do not need to reread everything. Picking a page or a chapter you liked and going through it again, perhaps with audio the second time, gives you a concentrated dose of fast, fluent reading that ordinary first-pass reading cannot.

## What Realistic Speed Actually Looks Like

It helps to have honest numbers, because unrealistic expectations are their own source of frustration. People assume they should read a foreign language nearly as fast as their own, get discouraged when they do not, and conclude they are failing. They are not. The gap is real and it is expected.

In your native language, adult silent reading runs somewhere around 230 to 260 words per minute for ordinary prose, with fiction usually a touch faster than dense non-fiction. That is the ceiling you are unconsciously comparing yourself to.

In a foreign language the picture is very different and depends heavily on your level. Beginning and lower-intermediate readers commonly read below 100 words per minute, sometimes well below. That is not a flaw; it is what early automaticity looks like. As proficiency rises, the gap shrinks. Even highly fluent bilinguals, people who read both languages comfortably every day, tend to read their second language around 30 percent slower than their first. One set of measurements put fluent bilingual reading near 185 words per minute against a first-language baseline around 265. So even at a very high level, a meaningful gap remains, and that is completely normal.

Two things follow from this. First, your target is not native speed; it is steady improvement and comfortable reading. Closing the gap to "only 30 percent slower than my native language" would mean you read your second language fluently. Second, the gains are real and measurable. Recall that 31 percent improvement over about 30 weeks of extensive reading. Speed responds to volume, reliably, as long as you keep reading at the right level.

If you feel completely stuck, reading the same amount without getting faster, that is often less about reading speed specifically and more about the broader [intermediate plateau](/en/blog/intermediate-plateau-how-to-break-through/), where the strategies that got you this far stop producing visible gains and the fix is to change how, and how much, you read.

## What Does Not Work

It is worth being blunt about the shortcuts that do not deliver, because the market is full of them and they waste time you could spend reading.

**Classic speed-reading techniques.** The methods sold as "speed reading," such as suppressing your inner voice, using a finger or pointer to force your eyes faster, or training wider eye fixations to take in whole lines, do not hold up well even in your native language. Research on speed reading has repeatedly found that beyond a point, faster eye movement comes at the direct cost of comprehension; you are not reading faster, you are reading less of the text. In a foreign language the idea is even weaker, because your bottleneck is not eye speed at all. It is word recognition and comprehension. Forcing your eyes across a page faster than your brain can recognize the words just means you understand less. You cannot speed-read your way around vocabulary you do not know automatically.

**Skipping comprehension to "cover more ground."** Some learners try to push through pages quickly without really understanding them, on the theory that exposure alone will help. Exposure helps only when there is comprehension attached. Reading words you do not understand does not build the form-to-meaning connections that create automaticity; it just moves your eyes. Volume matters, but it has to be comprehended volume.

**Translation apps that pull you out of the text.** Tools that require you to copy a sentence, switch apps, and read a full translation can be worse for flow than a paper dictionary, because the context switch is so heavy. The goal is to stay in the text. In-place, tap-to-reveal help supports reading; app-switching interrupts it.

**Grinding through texts that are too hard.** The most common self-inflicted slowdown is reading material far above your level out of ambition or impatience. It feels productive because it is difficult, but difficulty is not the same as progress. A text you decode one word at a time gives you almost no automaticity-building repetition. You will read faster, sooner, by reading easier things in greater quantity.

The pattern across all of these is the same: they try to manipulate the symptom (slow reading) instead of building the cause (automatic comprehension). That never works for long.

## The Bottom Line

There is no trick for reading faster in a foreign language, and anyone selling one is selling the symptom rather than the cause. Speed is not a skill you train directly. It is what happens automatically when word recognition becomes effortless and comprehension stops requiring conscious work.

That means the real instructions are short. Read a lot, at a level where you understand around 98 percent of the words, so that common words become automatic through sheer repetition. Use audio to pace your eyes and connect text to sound. Tolerate small unknowns instead of stopping for every word, and use fast in-place lookups when you do need help. Reread passages you enjoyed to feel what fluent reading is like. And set your expectations honestly: even fluent bilinguals read their second language slower than their first, so your target is steady improvement, not native speed.

Do that consistently and the speed comes on its own. Not because you forced your eyes faster, but because, page by page and book by book, the language stopped fighting you.
