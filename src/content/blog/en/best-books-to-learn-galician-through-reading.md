# Best Books to Learn Galician Through Reading: A Level-by-Level Guide from Beginner to Advanced

Galician, *galego*, to its roughly two and a half million speakers, is one of the most rewarding and least-known languages an English speaker can pick up. It is the Romance language of Galicia, the green, Atlantic-facing corner of north-west Spain that sits just above Portugal and ends at Cape Finisterre, the "end of the earth" of medieval imagination. It is co-official there alongside Spanish, it has a literary tradition stretching back eight centuries, and it carries a quiet emotional weight: for much of the twentieth century, writing in Galician at all was an act of cultural survival.

For the foreign reader, Galician comes with an unusual advantage and an unusual frustration, and you should understand both before you choose a book. The advantage is that Galician is extraordinarily accessible if you already read Spanish or Portuguese, not "somewhat similar," but genuinely a sister language, sharing a single medieval ancestor with Portuguese and centuries of contact with Spanish. The frustration is that, precisely because Galician is a smaller language, the kind of graded readers and learner-tiered material that exists for Spanish or French simply does not exist in any depth for Galician. You will mostly be reading real books, written for native readers, sooner than you might in a bigger language.

This guide is built around that reality. We organise real, verifiable Galician books by approximate CEFR level (A1/A2, B1/B2, C1+), explain why each one works for a learner and what to watch out for, and, crucially, spend time on the Spanish-and-Portuguese head-start, because for most readers it changes which book you should open first. Every title and author below is a real Galician work; nothing here is invented to fill a list.

A note on method before we begin: reading a real book in a language you are still learning only works if you are not constantly stuck. That is what tools like [parallel reading](/en/blog/parallel-reading-honest-guide/), where a translation sits beside the original sentence by sentence, are for, and we will come back to how to use them well. Let us start with the head-start, because for Galician it is the most important thing on the page.

## The Spanish and Portuguese Head-Start

If there is one thing to take away from this guide, it is this: your existing Romance languages matter more for Galician than for almost any other language you could choose.

### Why Galician Is So Accessible

Galician and Portuguese are not merely related, they are, historically, the same language. In the Middle Ages, from roughly the twelfth to the fourteenth centuries, the north-west of the Iberian Peninsula shared a single written standard now called **Galician-Portuguese**. This was a literary language of real prestige: more than 1,700 *cantigas*, lyric poems, love songs, songs of friendship, and biting satirical verse, survive from the troubadours of the period, and for a time even Castilian-speaking kings wrote their poetry in it. Only after the Kingdom of Portugal consolidated as an independent state did the two branches begin to drift apart, with the standards diverging across the late medieval and early modern centuries.

The practical consequence is that modern Galician and modern Portuguese remain highly mutually intelligible. A reader comfortable in Portuguese can often follow a Galician page with very little friction; the vocabulary overlaps enormously, the grammar runs in parallel, and many of the differences are matters of spelling convention and pronunciation rather than substance. If your Portuguese is solid, Galician is close to a free language.

Spanish gives you a slightly different, but still enormous, head-start. Galician and Spanish (Castilian) are separate Romance languages, but they have lived side by side in Galicia for centuries, sharing a peninsula, a great deal of Latin-derived vocabulary, and, since most Galician speakers are bilingual, a constant flow of mutual influence. For a Spanish reader, Galician feels like Spanish seen at an angle: the cognates are dense, the sentence shapes are familiar, and the leap is far smaller than from Spanish to, say, French or Italian.

### How Galician Differs from Both

The head-start is real, but Galician is its own language, and the differences are exactly where a learner trips. A few of the most useful to know before you read:

- **It is not "Spanish with a Portuguese accent."** Galician has its own grammar, its own standard orthography (set by the Real Academia Galega), and a distinct everyday vocabulary. Words you "know" from Spanish are sometimes false friends.
- **Spelling and small function words.** Galician uses forms a Spanish reader has to learn to recognise on sight, the definite articles *o, a, os, as* (rather than Spanish *el, la, los, las*), the conjunction *e* for "and," *non* for "no/not," *moi* for "very." None are hard, but they recur on every line, so learning the dozen most frequent ones first pays off immediately.
- **Where it leans toward Portuguese.** Many features that look "Portuguese" to a Spanish reader, certain vowel patterns, the *-ón/-ões*-type endings, vocabulary like *fillo* (son) against Spanish *hijo*, are simply Galician being itself.
- **Pronunciation and the *gheada*.** Galician has sounds and regional features (such as the *gheada*, where the *g* is pronounced as a breathy aspirate in parts of Galicia) that you will not fully grasp from text alone. This is a strong argument for choosing books with audio when you can.

## A1-A2: Your First Steps in Galician

At the absolute beginning, the goal is not literary depth. It is to spend time inside Galician sentences that are short, concrete, and, ideally, ones whose shape you can half-guess from Spanish or Portuguese. You want high-frequency words, simple present and past tenses, and stories you can follow even when individual words escape you. Honesty compels a caveat here that applies to all of Galician: there is very little material written *for learners* at a controlled level. So the A1-A2 strategy is to lean on two things, books you already know in another language, and children's literature, and to lean hard on translation support.

### O Principiño (Antoine de Saint-Exupéry, trans. Carlos Casares)

The single best first book in Galician is the one we recommend as a first book in almost every language: **O Principiño**, the Galician translation of Saint-Exupéry's *Le Petit Prince*. The Galician version, translated by the major Galician writer Carlos Casares and first published by Editorial Galaxia in 1972, is itself a small landmark, an early and important translated text in modern Galician children's literature, and one that has been reprinted continuously for decades.

Why it works at A1-A2: the prose is short, the vocabulary is concrete and emotional rather than technical, and, decisively, you probably already know the story. When you have read *The Little Prince* before in any language, your brain spends its energy on the Galician itself rather than on plot. The famous lines ("o esencial é invisible aos ollos") become anchors you can recognise and remember.

What to watch for: it is still a real translation of a real literary text, not a graded reader, so a true beginner will need the original-plus-translation crutch on most sentences at first. That is fine, that is the method. Use it.

### Galician Folk Tales: Contos Populares

After (or alongside) *O Principiño*, the natural next reservoir of accessible Galician is the country's own oral tradition: the *contos populares*, the folk tales. These are short, self-contained, and built on the universal grammar of folklore, the clever fox and the wolf, tasks and tricks, three brothers and a test, which means you are rarely lost about what is happening even when the words are new.

A concrete, verifiable starting point is **Contos populares da provincia de Lugo**, a collection first published in 1963. It was gathered through schools in the province of Lugo and transcribed by the poet Bernardino Graña during his time at the Colexio Fingoi, with a prologue by the scholar Ricardo Carballo Calero; it remains a standard reference for Galician oral narrative. The tales draw on the same deep well as Aesop, the medieval animal epic of the fox and the wolf, and the *Thousand and One Nights*, recast in a Galician voice.

Why folk tales work for beginners: each story is short enough to finish in a sitting, the structures repeat (so vocabulary recurs and sticks), and the register is plain, spoken Galician rather than elaborate literary prose. What to watch for: these are transcribed oral tales, so you will meet dialect colour, regional words, and an unfussy, sometimes elliptical style, treat the strange bits as flavour, not as something you must fully decode on a first pass.

### A Note on the Children's and Young-Adult Shelf

If you want more contemporary, accessible Galician once *O Principiño* and the folk tales feel comfortable, the children's and young-adult shelf is where modern Galician is at its most learner-friendly, modern spelling, present-day vocabulary, fast plots. A reliable name here is **Ledicia Costas**, one of the most successful living Galician writers for young readers; her **Escarlatina, a cociñeira defunta** (2014), a darkly funny story about a boy whose tenth birthday present is a coffin containing a nineteenth-century cook he must bring back to life, won Spain's National Prize for Children's and Young People's Literature in 2015 and is written in clear, contemporary Galician. It sits at the upper edge of this band, reaching toward B1, which makes it a good bridge into the next level.

## B1-B2: Building Real Reading Fluency

This is the level where Galician opens up, and where, thanks to the Romance head-start, many readers arrive faster than they expect. At B1-B2 you can handle real adult fiction with translation support: full past-tense narration, dialogue, figurative language, and chapters rather than pages. The books below are also, not coincidentally, among the most beloved and accessible works of modern Galician literature, which is exactly what you want, prose written to be read widely, not to show off.

### Memorias dun neno labrego (Xosé Neira Vilas)

If you read one twentieth-century Galician book, make it **Memorias dun neno labrego**, "Memoirs of a Peasant Boy." Written by Xosé Neira Vilas and first published in 1961 in Argentina, where so much of Galician culture lived in exile, it is the most widely read work in the Galician language; by 2015 it had sold more than 600,000 copies, and it is a fixture of the school curriculum in Galicia.

The book is narrated by Balbino, a poor farm boy in rural Galicia, in a series of short, first-person reflections on his childhood, hunger, school, friendship, injustice, the smallness and largeness of village life. For a learner this structure is a gift: the chapters are brief and self-contained, the voice is plain and direct, and the emotional clarity carries you through any vocabulary you do not yet have. It is also a humane, moving book on its own terms, which keeps you reading. What to watch for: the rural setting brings country and farming vocabulary, and the language has a mid-twentieth-century, regional texture, but the sentences themselves are simple, which is why it works so well at B1.

### O lapis do carpinteiro (Manuel Rivas)

Manuel Rivas is the most internationally visible living Galician writer, and **O lapis do carpinteiro** ("The Carpenter's Pencil," 1998) is his most translated novel, in fact the most translated work in the history of Galician literature. It tells a love story set against the Spanish Civil War: Daniel da Barca, a republican doctor imprisoned in Santiago de Compostela, and Marisa Mallo, the daughter of a right-wing family. It won the Spanish Critics' Prize and the prize of the Galician-language writers' association in 1998.

For the learner, Rivas offers literary Galician at its most readable: short, the prose lyrical but not dense, the plot strong enough to pull you forward. Because it has been translated into many languages, you can also find a high-quality English version to read alongside it, ideal for parallel reading. What to watch for: the historical setting assumes some background on the Civil War, and Rivas's prose is genuinely literary, with imagery and shifts in time that ask more of you than Neira Vilas does. This is mid-to-upper B2 territory, and a satisfying step up.

### ¿Que me queres, amor? (Manuel Rivas)

If a whole novel feels like too much at once, start with Rivas's short stories. **¿Que me queres, amor?** (first published in 1995 by Editorial Galaxia) is a collection of sixteen stories and an ideal learner format: you finish a complete piece in one sitting, get the satisfaction of a whole arc, and can stop without losing a thread. Its most famous story, **A lingua das bolboretas** ("The Language of Butterflies"), about a boy and his teacher in the months before the Civil War, was adapted into the acclaimed film *La lengua de las mariposas*.

Why short stories suit this level: variety keeps vocabulary fresh, the self-contained structure lets you re-read a single story until it clicks, and Rivas's everyday-but-poetic register is some of the most pleasurable contemporary Galician prose to read. What to watch for: the stories range in difficulty and mood, and some lean impressionistic, but you can simply pick the ones that grip you and skip the rest, a freedom a novel does not give you.

## C1+: Galician at Full Depth

At the advanced level you can read Galician literature on its own terms, denser prose, older language, stylistic ambition, and the canonical works that define the tradition. Some of these are demanding even for native readers, so treat them as goals and rewards rather than the place to begin.

### Cantares Gallegos and Follas Novas (Rosalía de Castro)

No account of Galician reading is complete without **Rosalía de Castro**, the towering figure of the *Rexurdimento*, the nineteenth-century renaissance of Galician as a literary language, and, by common consent, the mother of modern Galician literature. Her **Cantares gallegos** (1863) was the first major work written in Galician in centuries, a turning point that pulled the language out of the purely oral world it had been confined to since the late Middle Ages; her **Follas novas** (1880) is the more inward, melancholy companion volume, ranging from popular-rooted lyric to the pain of emigration. Together they are foundational texts of the entire tradition.

A clear-eyed warning for learners: this is nineteenth-century poetry, written before the standardisation of modern Galician spelling, in a literary and at times archaic register. The reward, reading the very poems that revived the language, is immense, but the difficulty is real. Approach Rosalía once your everyday Galician is comfortable, lean heavily on facing translations, and read for the music and the meaning of a single poem at a time rather than trying to march through the book. This is C1+ work, and worth every bit of effort.

### Cousas and Os dous de sempre (Castelao)

**Alfonso Daniel Rodríguez Castelao**, writer, artist, doctor and the central figure of twentieth-century Galician nationalism, left two works every advanced reader should know. **Cousas** ("Things," 1926) is a collection of short prose pieces, each paired with one of his own drawings: tiny, sharp portraits of Galician life that are accessible in length but reward attention to their irony and tenderness. **Os dous de sempre** (1934), his only novel, follows two contrasting characters, Pedriño and Rañolas, across forty-four illustrated chapters, and is a small classic of Galician prose.

For the learner, *Cousas* is the better entry point: its pieces are short and its drawings give you context, even though the wit and the early-twentieth-century language put it at C1. What to watch for: Castelao writes in the Galician of his time and place, with regional colour and a compressed, allusive style that assumes the reader is already inside the culture.

### Álvaro Cunqueiro

For the most purely literary pleasure at the top of the difficulty range, there is **Álvaro Cunqueiro** (1911-1981), a writer of luminous, fantastical prose. His **Merlín e familia** (1955) reimagines the Arthurian magician as a retired Merlin living in the Galician woods, attended by his page, a book of fables, marvels and digressions that is one of the glories of Galician literature. Cunqueiro began as a poet, and it shows: his sentences are rich, ornate, and full of invented detail.

This is the deep end. Cunqueiro's vocabulary is vast, his syntax leisurely and elaborate, and his world-building dense with allusion, exactly the qualities that make him beloved and that make him hard. Save him for when reading Galician is a pleasure rather than a struggle, and even then expect to slow down. As a destination, though, he is one of the best reasons to learn the language at all.

## Choosing Your First Galician Book

With the levels laid out, the practical question is simply: where do *you* start? A few honest rules of thumb.

If you already read Portuguese well, you can skip much of the on-ramp. Start with **Memorias dun neno labrego** or Rivas's **¿Que me queres, amor?** directly; you will be surprised how little stands in your way. If you read Spanish, begin with **O Principiño** and the **contos populares** to absorb the recurring function words and spelling that differ from Castilian, then move quickly to Neira Vilas. If Galician is your first Romance language, or your first foreign language at all, do not rush: spend real time with *O Principiño* and folk tales, and read our guide to choosing a [first book in a foreign language](/en/blog/first-book-in-a-foreign-language/) before you commit.

Above all, match the book to your level rather than your ambition. The most common way to fail at reading in a new language is to start with the masterpiece, Rosalía or Cunqueiro, bounce off it, and conclude you "can't read Galician." You can; you just started too high. Our [best books by language level](/en/blog/best-books-by-language-level/) guide is built around exactly this principle, and it applies to Galician with unusual force given how thin the graded-reader middle ground is. Pick the easy book, finish it, and let momentum do the rest.

One more thing worth saying plainly: because Galician has spent so long as a spoken language under pressure, hearing it matters. The *gheada*, the open and closed vowels, the rhythm of a real Galician sentence, these live in sound, not on the page. Wherever you can, read with audio, so that the language you are decoding with your eyes is also one you can begin to hear.

## The Bottom Line

Galician rewards readers more generously than its small size suggests. If you already know Spanish or Portuguese, you are not starting from zero, you are starting from a running leap, because Galician shares a medieval ancestor with Portuguese and centuries of life beside Spanish. The catch is that you will be reading real books, written for native readers, earlier than in a bigger language, because the graded-reader middle ground barely exists.

So choose deliberately. Begin with **O Principiño** and the **contos populares** to settle into the language's shape; move to **Memorias dun neno labrego** and Manuel Rivas's **O lapis do carpinteiro** and **¿Que me queres, amor?** for your first real fluency; and keep **Rosalía de Castro**, **Castelao**, and **Álvaro Cunqueiro** as the summits you climb once Galician feels like a pleasure rather than a puzzle. Read at the right level, lean on parallel text and audio to stay unstuck, and let one finished book lead to the next. That is how the language opens, not all at once, but one *conto* at a time.
