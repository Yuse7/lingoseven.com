Catalan is one of the most quietly accessible languages an English speaker can take up, and one of the most misunderstood. It is not a dialect of Spanish. It is a full Romance language in its own right, spoken by some millions of people across Catalonia, the Valencian Community, the Balearic Islands, the eastern strip of Aragon, the French region of Roussillon, and the sovereign state of Andorra, where it is the only official language. It has its own grammar, its own literary tradition stretching back to the medieval troubadours and the philosopher Ramon Llull, and its own living body of contemporary fiction.

What makes Catalan especially friendly to readers is its position on the map of Romance languages. It sits between Spanish and French, not as a blend of the two, but as a third sibling that shares a great deal with both. If you have studied Spanish, Catalan vocabulary will feel familiar nine words out of ten. If you have studied French, you will recognise much of the rest: words like *finestra* (window, compare French *fenêtre*), *menjar* (to eat, compare *manger*), and *taula* (table, compare *table*) that look nothing like their Spanish equivalents but slot neatly into a French-trained eye. Know either language and reading Catalan becomes a matter of bridging a gap, not crossing an ocean.

This guide curates real, verifiable Catalan books for learners, organised by the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR) levels, A1/A2 for beginners, B1/B2 for intermediate readers, and C1+ for advanced ones. Every title and author here is genuine and checkable. For each, you will find the level it suits, why it works for learners, and what to watch for so you do not pick up a book that will quietly defeat you. There is also a section on how to convert a Spanish or French head-start into momentum, and how to choose your very first Catalan book.

A note before we begin: reading is the single most efficient way to grow a foreign vocabulary, but only if the book is roughly at your level. Too easy and you learn nothing new; too hard and you stall on the second page. The whole point of organising by level is to keep you in the productive middle, stretched, but not drowning.

## Why Catalan Is More Approachable Than It Looks

People often assume Catalan must be a niche, difficult undertaking simply because it is less famous than Spanish or French. The opposite is closer to the truth for a reader. The language is highly regular in spelling, the literary tradition is rich and well-translated, and, crucially, you are extremely unlikely to come to it cold.

### The Spanish and French head-start

Catalan is a Western Romance language, and its lexicon overlaps heavily with both of its better-known neighbours. The result is that almost nobody learns to read Catalan from a true zero. If you already read Spanish, you are perhaps eighty per cent of the way to recognising Catalan vocabulary on sight; the grammar is close enough that the conjugation patterns will feel like variations on a theme you already know. If your second language is French, you will find Catalan's vocabulary leaning your way in exactly the places where it diverges from Spanish, and French speakers tend to find Catalan pronunciation and its weak vowels less alien than Spanish speakers do.

What you actually have to learn is a relatively short list of distinctively Catalan features: the definite articles *el*, *la*, *els*, *les*; the personal article *en* and *na* before names; the rich system of weak pronouns (*em*, *et*, *es*, *ens*, *hi*, *ho*, *en*); past tenses formed with *anar* plus the infinitive (the periphrastic preterite, which trips up everyone at first, *vaig anar* means "I went," not "I am going"); and the spelling conventions, including the famous *l·l* (the *ela geminada*) and the distinction between open and closed accents. None of this is hard. It is just *specific*, and a few weeks of reading with translation support fixes most of it.

The honest caveat: among Romance languages, Catalan is on the easier end *for someone who already knows a Romance language*. If Catalan would be your very first foray into the family, with no Spanish, French, Italian, or Portuguese behind you, it is no easier than learning Spanish from scratch, it is simply less supported, with fewer beginner textbooks and graded readers on the market. That scarcity is the single biggest practical obstacle, and it is exactly the gap that parallel reading fills.

### Choosing your first Catalan book

The mistake almost every learner makes is reaching straight for the famous novel, the one everyone calls "the great Catalan book", and bouncing off it. Literary prestige and reading difficulty are not the same axis. Mercè Rodoreda's *La plaça del Diamant* is a masterpiece, but its narrator's stream-of-consciousness voice is genuinely demanding even for natives. Starting there is like learning to swim by jumping off a pier.

A better rule: choose your first book by the difficulty of its *language*, not the prestige of its *name*. For most learners that means starting with material written for children or for language students, folk tales, *El petit príncep*, a children's adventure novel, where sentences are short, vocabulary is concrete, and the plot pulls you forward. You graduate to the literary canon later, when the mechanics of reading have stopped costing you effort. We walk through exactly this progression in our guide to [reading your first book in a foreign language](/en/blog/first-book-in-a-foreign-language/), and the general principle of matching books to your level is the subject of our [level-by-level reading guide](/en/blog/best-books-by-language-level/).

## Beginner Level (A1-A2): Building Your First Foothold

At A1 and A2 you know the present tense, basic everyday vocabulary, and you can follow short, concrete sentences. You do not yet have the patience for long subordinate clauses or literary abstraction. The goal at this stage is volume of easy input, read a lot of simple Catalan so the high-frequency words (*que*, *de*, *amb*, *però*, *fer*, *tenir*, *anar*) become automatic.

### Catalan folk tales, *rondalles*

The single best place to start reading Catalan is its folk-tale tradition, the *rondalles*. These are short, self-contained stories with the comforting repetitive structure of fairy tales the world over: a clear problem, a journey, a clever resolution. The vocabulary is concrete and the sentences are short, which is exactly what a beginner needs.

There are several genuine, well-known collections. The most monumental is the *Aplec de rondaies mallorquines* gathered by the Majorcan priest and linguist Antoni Maria Alcover (writing under the pen name "Jordi des Racó") from 1896 onward, a vast, lively body of oral tales from Mallorca, considered one of the largest collections of directly recorded folklore anywhere. On the mainland, the poet Jacint Verdaguer published his own collection of *Rondalles* in 1905, retelling tales he had gathered from the Pyrenees and the Osona region in his own polished prose. Earlier still, Francesc Maspons i Labrós produced the three-volume *Lo Rondallayre* (1871-1875), the first folk-tale collection published in Catalan in book form.

**Why it works:** short pieces mean frequent finishing-points, which keeps motivation high. The recurring fairy-tale formulas teach you sentence patterns through sheer repetition. And because these are oral tales written down, the language is close to everyday speech.

**What to watch for:** the Mallorcan *rondaies* in particular preserve the island's Balearic dialect, including the salat article (*es*, *sa* instead of *el*, *la*) and vocabulary you will not meet in standard mainland Catalan. That is wonderful cultural texture but a distraction for a first-week beginner. If you want the standard language, start with Verdaguer's mainland *Rondalles* or a modern standardised collection, and treat the Mallorcan tales as a delightful detour once you have your feet under you.

### *El petit príncep* (Antoine de Saint-Exupéry)

Saint-Exupéry's *The Little Prince* exists in excellent Catalan translation, *El petit príncep*, with versions including those by Joan Xancó and, more recently, by Anna and Enric Casassas. As the second most translated book in the world after the Bible, it is the universal first reader, and for good reason. The prose is simple, the sentences are short, and the philosophical weight sits underneath a surface that a beginner can actually decode.

**Why it works:** if you have read it before in English or another language, you already know the plot, which frees up enormous mental capacity for the language itself. The vocabulary is everyday and the tone is gentle. It is also short enough to finish in a few sittings, and finishing your first book in a new language is a genuine confidence event.

**What to watch for:** do not let the children's-book packaging fool you into expecting *no* challenge, the fox's famous speeches drift into abstraction, and a few passages are more poetic than plain. That is fine; let yourself skim the harder paragraphs and savour the easy ones. The point at A2 is momentum.

### *El zoo d'en Pitus* (Sebastià Sorribas)

If you want a longer beginner read with a real plot, *El zoo d'en Pitus* by Sebastià Sorribas i Roig is a Catalan children's classic and a frequent set text in schools. First published in 1966, it won the Josep Maria Folch i Torres prize and has run to dozens of editions. The story follows a group of neighbourhood children who build a makeshift zoo to raise money so a sick friend can travel to Sweden for treatment, a warm tale of friendship and collective effort.

**Why it works:** it is written for young Catalan readers, so the grammar is standard and clean, the vocabulary is contemporary and concrete, and the chapters are short. Unlike a folk-tale collection, it sustains a single narrative, which trains you to hold characters and plot threads across a whole book, a different and necessary reading muscle.

**What to watch for:** children's dialogue includes colloquialisms and the occasional dated expression, since the book is now decades old. Nothing serious, but expect a few words your dictionary frames as informal. This is a strong "second book" once *El petit príncep* has warmed you up.

## Intermediate Level (B1-B2): Reading Real Catalan Literature

By B1 and B2 you can handle past tenses, longer sentences, and a degree of abstraction. This is the level where Catalan literature genuinely opens up, where you stop reading material written *for* learners and start reading books written for adult Catalan readers. It is also the most satisfying stretch, because the payoff is real literature rather than simplified text.

### *La plaça del Diamant* (Mercè Rodoreda)

This is the one. *La plaça del Diamant* (1962), translated into English as *The Time of the Doves*, is widely regarded as the central novel of modern Catalan literature and one of the finest works of fiction produced in Spain since the Civil War. It has been translated into more than thirty languages. It follows Natàlia, nicknamed Colometa, "little dove", a young working-class woman in Barcelona's Gràcia district, through marriage, motherhood, the Spanish Civil War, and its grinding aftermath, all narrated in her own voice.

**Why it works:** the vocabulary is largely everyday and domestic, home, children, food, work, the neighbourhood, rather than ornate or technical, which keeps it within B2 reach. The emotional power is immense, and that pull carries you through pages you might otherwise find slow. Finishing the national novel in the original is also a real milestone, the kind that makes the whole project of learning Catalan feel worthwhile.

**What to watch for:** be honest with yourself about the narrative voice. Rodoreda writes Natàlia's interior monologue in a flowing, associative, sometimes ungrammatical-by-design stream that mirrors how a mind actually wanders. It is beautiful, but it means the prose does not always give you the clean subject-verb-object scaffolding a learner leans on. This is a high-B2 book, not an entry-level one. Read it after you have a couple of easier novels behind you, and lean on a parallel translation when her sentences blur, which they will, on purpose. It is genuinely a better book in the original; the rhythm of Colometa's Catalan does not fully survive translation, a phenomenon we explore in our piece on [books that are better in their original language](/en/blog/books-better-in-original-language/).

### Quim Monzó, short stories

Quim Monzó (born Barcelona, 1952) is the most celebrated living Catalan short-story writer, an ironic, sharp, often surreal observer of modern life. His collections, including *El perquè de tot plegat* (1993, translated as *Why, Why, Why?*) and the stories gathered in English as *A Thousand Morons* and *Guadalajara*, are widely translated and award-laden. For a learner, short fiction is a structural gift: each story is a fresh start, a small, complete world you can finish in one sitting.

**Why it works:** the bite-sized format means you can read a whole piece in a single session, which is enormously motivating and lets you reread the same story to consolidate vocabulary without committing to hundreds of pages. Monzó's contemporary settings mean the vocabulary is current and useful, the Catalan of cities, offices, relationships, and everyday absurdity. His sentences are also cleaner and more modern than Rodoreda's.

**What to watch for:** the difficulty in Monzó is rarely the words and often the wit. His stories turn on irony, twist endings, and cultural reference, so the literal meaning of a sentence and its actual *point* can diverge. If a story's ending leaves you confused, the problem is usually the joke, not the grammar, reread, and let the second pass land. That is good practice anyway.

### Building toward B2 with a translation in view

A practical tactic at this level is to read a Catalan novel you have already read in English or Spanish. Knowing the plot in advance lifts a huge load off comprehension and lets you focus on *how* Catalan says things. Translated international fiction, contemporary Catalan crime novels, and young-adult titles all work well here. The principle of pairing a known story with a new language is the engine behind parallel reading, which we lay out in full in our [honest guide to parallel reading](/en/blog/parallel-reading-honest-guide/).

## Advanced Level (C1+): The Catalan Canon

At C1 and above you can read for pleasure, tolerate ambiguity, and handle long, syntactically complex sentences, literary register, and historical or regional vocabulary. This is where the deep canon of Catalan literature awaits, books that are demanding even for educated native speakers, and all the more rewarding for it.

### *El quadern gris* (Josep Pla)

*El quadern gris* (*The Grey Notebook*, 1966) is one of the towering achievements of twentieth-century Catalan prose, and for many readers the single greatest. It is presented as the diary Josep Pla kept as a young man in 1918-1920, reworked decades later by the established writer he became, a portrait of a mind awakening to the world and to its own vocation. Pla is celebrated as the supreme prose stylist of modern Catalan, prized for clarity, irony, and the relentless pursuit of the exact word. An English translation by Peter Bush appeared in 2014.

**Why it works for advanced learners:** Pla's sentences are models of precision, and his diaristic structure means you can read it in short, self-contained entries rather than tracking a single long plot. His subject, ordinary life in early-twentieth-century Catalonia, observed with a clear eye, is endlessly absorbing, and the prose rewards the slow, attentive reading that advanced study encourages.

**What to watch for:** the deceptively "simple" style is a trap. Pla's clarity is achieved through an enormous, precise vocabulary, he is famous for finding the one *exact* adjective, so you will meet a great many low-frequency words used with surgical intent. Expect to keep a dictionary close. The rewards are proportionate.

### *Jo confesso* (Jaume Cabré)

Jaume Cabré's *Jo confesso* (*Confessions*, 2011) is a contemporary epic, roughly a thousand pages, and one of the most internationally successful Catalan novels of recent decades, translated into more than twenty languages. It is the life-confession of Adrià Ardèvol, a brilliant Barcelona collector, woven together with meditations on evil across history, a cursed Renaissance violin, the Inquisition, and the Holocaust. It won the Catalan Critics' Award and the Serra d'Or Critics' Prize.

**Why it works for advanced learners:** the sheer length means that once you adapt to Cabré's voice, you have hundreds of pages of *consistent* style ahead of you, and that sustained immersion does wonders for fluency. It is a genuine page-turner despite its ambition, which keeps you reading.

**What to watch for:** this is a hard book, and not only because of its size. Cabré shifts narrative voice mid-sentence, sometimes mid-clause, sliding between first and third person and between time periods without warning, as a deliberate technique. It is dazzling once you tune in and bewildering before you do. Reserve this for solid C1 or above, and do not make it your first long Catalan novel. If a thousand pages of stylistic acrobatics sounds like a lot, it is; treat it as a goal to read *toward*, not a place to start.

### *Solitud* (Víctor Català)

*Solitud* (1905) by Víctor Català, the pen name of Caterina Albert, is regarded as the most important Catalan novel written before the Civil War and a landmark of Catalan *Modernisme*. It follows Mila, a young woman who moves with her husband to an isolated mountain hermitage and confronts the harshness of the landscape, the people around her, and her own emerging sense of self. It won the Fastenrath prize in 1909 and stands as a foundational exploration of female identity by a woman writer in Catalan.

**Why it works for advanced learners:** it is a relatively compact novel of real psychological depth and atmospheric power, and its place at the head of the modern canon makes reading it a genuinely meaningful accomplishment. The natural setting yields rich, vivid descriptive vocabulary.

**What to watch for:** the prose is dense, the register is early-twentieth-century literary, and the rural setting brings vocabulary, plants, weather, mountain life, religion, that is concrete but uncommon. The style is more demanding than its modest length suggests. This is a C1+ book, best approached once you are comfortable reading older literary Catalan.

## Choosing Between Spanish and Catalan, and Reading Both

Many learners arrive at Catalan through Spanish, and a fair question is whether to study them in parallel or in sequence. The two are close enough that learning both at once risks confusion, you will reach for a Spanish word in a Catalan sentence and vice versa. The cleaner path is to get one to a solid intermediate footing first, then add the other, at which point the overlap becomes pure leverage rather than interference.

## The Bottom Line

Catalan rewards readers more generously than its modest fame suggests. It is a full Romance language with a deep literary tradition, and it sits at an unusually convenient crossroads: know Spanish or French, or any Romance language, and you start the reading journey most of the way there, recognising the bulk of the vocabulary on sight and needing only to absorb a short list of distinctively Catalan features.

The path is the same one that works for any language. Start below the prestige line, with folk tales, *El petit príncep*, and *El zoo d'en Pitus*, where the language is concrete and the wins come fast. Move up to the heart of the modern canon, Rodoreda's *La plaça del Diamant* and Monzó's sharp short stories, once past tenses and longer sentences no longer slow you down. And save Pla's *El quadern gris*, Cabré's vast *Jo confesso*, and Català's *Solitud* for the point where reading Catalan has become a pleasure rather than a puzzle. Match the book to your level, read more than you look up, and let a parallel translation carry you over the sentences that would otherwise stop you. Do that, and the great Catalan novel you could not finish today becomes the one you finish next year, in the original, the way it was written to be read.
