# Best Books to Learn Romanian Through Reading: A Level-by-Level Guide From Beginner to Advanced

Romanian sits in an unusual position among the languages you might choose to learn through reading. It is a Romance language -- a direct descendant of the Latin carried into the eastern Balkans by Roman colonists -- which means that if you already read any French, Spanish, Italian, or Portuguese, large stretches of a Romanian page will feel half-familiar before you have studied a single rule. Words like *important*, *naţiune*, *libertate*, *animal*, and *student* need no explanation. The U.S. Foreign Service Institute classifies Romanian as a Category I language, the easiest tier for English speakers, requiring roughly 600 to 750 hours of study to reach professional proficiency -- the same bracket as Spanish and French.

And yet Romanian is the odd one out in that family, and reading reveals exactly why. It is the only major Romance language to have preserved a working noun-case system, so the same word changes shape depending on its role in the sentence. It absorbed a substantial Slavic vocabulary over centuries of contact with neighbouring peoples -- by most estimates ten to fifteen percent of the everyday lexicon -- so alongside all those transparent Latin cognates you will meet words like *da* (yes), *prieten* (friend), and *iubire* (love) that owe nothing to Latin and will not be guessable from French or Italian. Reading is, in fact, one of the best ways to make peace with this double inheritance, because you encounter the cognates and the surprises in their natural habitat instead of memorising them on flashcards.

This guide curates real, verifiable Romanian books worth reading at each stage, organised by the CEFR levels -- A1/A2 for beginners, B1/B2 for intermediate readers, and C1+ for advanced ones. For every title you will find why it works as a learning text and, just as importantly, what to watch for, because the wrong book at the wrong moment is the fastest way to quit. We will be honest throughout: Romanian has fewer purpose-built learner materials than Spanish or French, and some of its most beloved classics are written in a language that even native speakers find archaic. Knowing that in advance is half the battle.

## Why Reading Works So Well for Romanian

Before the book list, it is worth understanding why reading is a particularly strong strategy for this specific language -- and where it needs support.

### The Cognate Advantage Is Real, but Uneven

When you read Romanian, the Latin core does a great deal of the heavy lifting. Abstract and academic vocabulary -- the language of news, essays, and formal prose -- is heavily Latinate and, since the nineteenth century, was deliberately re-Latinised and enriched with French and Italian borrowings. This is why a Romanian newspaper editorial can be startlingly readable for someone who knows another Romance language, while a folk tale about village life can be much harder: the everyday, concrete, domestic vocabulary is where the older Slavic and substrate words cluster.

The practical takeaway is that your reading difficulty in Romanian does not always track with what you would expect. A "simple" children's story about a peasant household may throw more unfamiliar words at you than a "complex" article about politics or science. Plan your reading accordingly, and do not be discouraged when a text aimed at children turns out to be harder than one aimed at adults.

### The Grammar You Have to See to Believe

Romanian keeps three cases (a merged nominative-accusative, a merged genitive-dative, and a vocative), three genders including a genuinely neuter class that behaves as masculine in the singular and feminine in the plural, and -- most distinctively -- a definite article that attaches to the *end* of the noun rather than standing in front of it. *Lup* is "wolf"; *lupul* is "the wolf." *Carte* is "book"; *cartea* is "the book." No amount of cognate-spotting prepares you for this, and it is exactly the kind of pattern that reading drills into you painlessly. After a few chapters you stop parsing the suffix consciously and simply recognise *omul*, *femeia*, *copilul* as "the man," "the woman," "the child." This is the strongest argument for reading over rote study in Romanian: the morphology is regular enough to internalise through exposure, and dense enough that exposure beats explanation.

### Where Parallel Text and Audio Close the Gap

The honest weakness of learning Romanian through reading is the thin supply of graded material. For Spanish or French you can buy a dozen well-made graded-reader series; for Romanian the shelf is much shorter. This is precisely where a parallel-text approach earns its keep. When you can read a Romanian sentence with an aligned English translation a tap away, almost any authentic text becomes a "graded" text -- you supply the scaffolding yourself, sentence by sentence, instead of waiting for a publisher to do it for you. Add synchronised native audio and you also fix Romanian's other reading trap: the spelling looks transparent but hides sounds (the letters *ă*, *â*, *î*, *ş/ș*, *ţ/ț*) that you will mispronounce silently in your head forever unless you hear them. ## A1-A2: Your First Romanian Books

At the beginner stage your goal is not literary appreciation -- it is volume. You want to read a large quantity of text you can mostly follow, so that the case endings, the suffixed articles, and the high-frequency vocabulary become automatic. Two kinds of book serve this best: purpose-built bilingual readers, and one famous translated story that nearly every learner of every language passes through.

### Bilingual Short-Story Collections for Learners

The single most useful category for an absolute beginner is the bilingual graded reader, and here it must be said plainly: the selection in Romanian is modest. The standout is **"25 Short Stories for Romanian Learners" by Alina Vasile**, subtitled *25 Bilingual Stories for Beginners and Intermediate Learners*. It does exactly what the title promises -- short, controlled-vocabulary stories presented for learners, spanning a range of themes and difficulty so you can grow inside a single volume. For a true beginner this is close to ideal: the texts are short enough to finish in one sitting, which matters enormously for motivation, and the bilingual framing means you are never stranded.

**Why it works:** controlled vocabulary, short units, and immediate translation support remove the two things that kill beginners -- looking up every third word, and never finishing anything.

**What to watch for:** a single collection is not a curriculum. You will exhaust it quickly, and there is no deep back-catalogue of Romanian graded readers to graduate into the way there is for Spanish. Treat it as a launchpad, not a home. The well-known **StoryLearning / Teach Yourself "Short Stories in..." series by Olly Richards** is excellent and uses exactly the format beginners need, but note that, at the time of writing, it covers many languages without a dedicated Romanian volume -- so do not buy it expecting Romanian content. (If you are coming to Romanian from Italian, our [Italian reading guide](/en/blog/best-books-to-learn-italian-through-reading/) lists the parallel options on that side of the family.)

### "Micul Prinț" (The Little Prince)

Antoine de Saint-Exupéry's *The Little Prince* exists in Romanian as **"Micul Prinț,"** with several published translations -- including widely available editions by translators such as Ioana Pârvulescu (Editura Arthur) and others. There is a reason this slim book is the unofficial first reader for learners of almost every language: the sentences are short, the vocabulary is concrete and emotional rather than technical, the story is gentle, and -- crucially for a beginner -- you very likely already know the plot, which means you can read for language and lean on memory for comprehension.

**Why it works:** familiar story, simple syntax, manageable length. In Romanian it also quietly teaches you the suffixed article and the present tense in their most natural, repeated forms.

**What to watch for:** even "simple" literary Romanian uses the past tense and some constructions a textbook would not introduce so early. Read it with translation support rather than a dictionary, and accept that you will not catch every nuance on the first pass. That is fine -- the point is exposure, not mastery.

### Children's and Folk Material -- With a Caveat

It is tempting to reach for children's books and classic fairy tales at A2, and the impulse is sound, but Romanian carries a specific trap here that we address in full in the next section. In short: the most famous Romanian children's classics are written in a language that is partly archaic and dialectal, which makes them harder, not easier, than their target audience suggests. For now, prefer modern children's books and contemporary fairy-tale retellings over the nineteenth-century originals. Save the classics for when you can enjoy their flavour instead of fighting it.

## B1-B2: Reading Real Romanian

This is the stage where the language opens up. You have the core grammar, you recognise the high-frequency words on sight, and you can tolerate a page with a handful of unknowns. Now you can move from material written *for* learners to material written *for* Romanians -- which is where the literature actually lives. The intermediate years are also when Romania's great storytellers become accessible, so this is the richest section of the guide.

### Ion Creangă -- "Amintiri din copilărie" and "Povești"

No writer is more central to Romanian childhood than **Ion Creangă** (1837-1889), the schoolteacher-turned-storyteller whose memoir **"Amintiri din copilărie"** ("Childhood Memories") is regarded by critics as his masterpiece and one of the warmest first-person narratives in the language. His **"Povești"** ("Stories") -- including the celebrated *Povestea lui Harap-Alb* -- are pillars of the Romanian folk-tale tradition. Reading Creangă is, in a real sense, reading the Romanian soul: the humour, the rhythm of village speech, the moral logic of the fairy tale.

**Why it works:** the stories are vivid, funny, and culturally foundational; you will hear them referenced for the rest of your life with Romanian speakers. The narrative momentum pulls you through.

**What to watch for -- and this is the big one:** Creangă writes in a deliberately oral, regional Moldavian register that is *somewhat archaic* and full of dialectal words and idioms even native Romanians need glossed. Do not mistake "children's classic" for "beginner text." This is genuinely a B2-and-up read in its original form, and it is the clearest example in this whole guide of why concrete, rural, traditional Romanian is harder than abstract modern Romanian. Read it with strong translation support, and treat the archaic vocabulary as cultural seasoning rather than something you must actively acquire.

### Petre Ispirescu -- Romanian Fairy Tales

**Petre Ispirescu** (1830-1887) was the great collector of Romanian folk tales, and his **"Legende sau basmele românilor"** ("Legends or the Fairy Tales of the Romanians," first published 1872) gathered the canonical stories -- *Tinerețe fără bătrânețe și viață fără de moarte* ("Youth Without Age and Life Without Death"), *Prâslea cel voinic și merele de aur* ("Prâslea the Brave and the Golden Apples"), and many more. These are the bedrock myths of the culture, told in clear, repetitive, formulaic fairy-tale language.

**Why it works:** fairy-tale structure is forgiving for learners -- events repeat in threes, phrases recur, and the arc is predictable, so you can often infer meaning from pattern. Culturally, this is essential reading.

**What to watch for:** like Creangă, Ispirescu's language is nineteenth-century and carries folk and archaic vocabulary. The formulaic style helps, but you will still meet older verb forms and words that have faded from modern speech. Pick individual famous tales rather than reading the whole collection front-to-back, and let the recurring formulas teach you their own rhythm.

### Mihail Sebastian

For a bridge from folk material into modern literary prose, **Mihail Sebastian** (1907-1945) is one of the best choices a B2 reader can make. His novels -- including **"Accidentul"** ("The Accident") and the landmark **"De două mii de ani"** ("For Two Thousand Years," 1934) -- are written in a clear, elegant, French-influenced twentieth-century Romanian that is far closer to the language you are actually learning than anything by Creangă or Ispirescu. Sebastian was also a playwright; *Steaua fără nume* ("The Star Without a Name") is a gentle, much-loved comedy.

**Why it works:** modern, lucid syntax; rich but not obscure vocabulary; emotionally serious but accessible themes. This is the register that rewards your Romance-language cognate instincts most directly.

**What to watch for:** *De două mii de ani* is a profound, sometimes painful interwar meditation on Jewish identity in Romania; it is not heavy linguistically but it is heavy thematically. If you want to start lighter, the plays are an easier on-ramp than the novels.

### Mircea Eliade -- Short Fiction

**Mircea Eliade** (1907-1986), world-famous as a historian of religion, was also a major fiction writer, and his shorter work is a fine intermediate-to-advanced target. His semi-autobiographical novel **"Maitreyi"** (1933) and his fantastic novellas and stories -- the collection **"La țigănci"** ("With the Gypsy Girls," 1963) and **"Domnișoara Christina"** ("Miss Christina," 1936) among them -- blend everyday Bucharest realism with sudden eruptions of the uncanny.

**Why it works:** Eliade writes modern, twentieth-century Romanian with a strong narrative pull. Starting with a single novella rather than a full novel lets you test the waters at story length.

**What to watch for:** the "fantastic" stories deliberately blur reality, so comprehension difficulty is sometimes about *meaning*, not vocabulary -- you may understand every word and still wonder what just happened. That ambiguity is intentional; enjoy it rather than fighting it. Save the densest of his work for when you are firmly at B2+.

## C1 and Beyond: The Heights of Romanian Literature

At an advanced level you read for the writing itself, and Romanian rewards you with a literature out of all proportion to the size of its speaker community. These books are demanding -- some are demanding even for educated native readers -- so the guidance here is about matching the challenge to your stamina, not about easing you in.

### Liviu Rebreanu -- "Ion" and "Pădurea spânzuraților"

**Liviu Rebreanu** (1885-1944) is the founder of the modern Romanian novel. **"Ion"** (1920) is widely regarded as the first modern Romanian novel -- an unflinching, objective-realist portrait of peasant land-hunger in Transylvania -- while **"Pădurea spânzuraților"** ("The Forest of the Hanged," 1922) follows a Transylvanian officer's existential collapse on the Eastern Front of the First World War, a story drawn from the execution of Rebreanu's own brother.

**Why it works:** Rebreanu's prose is sober, controlled, and modern in syntax; this is "serious literature" without the linguistic obstacles of the nineteenth-century folk writers. The psychological depth makes the effort worthwhile.

**What to watch for:** the rural settings bring substantial concrete, regional, and period vocabulary -- exactly the harder, less cognate-friendly end of Romanian. These are long novels with weighty subject matter. Budget time, and do not start here unless you can already sustain reading for pleasure in Romanian.

### Mihai Eminescu -- Poetry

**Mihai Eminescu** (1850-1889) is Romania's national poet -- a figure to Romanian culture roughly what Shakespeare is to English, often called the godfather of the modern literary language. His masterpiece **"Luceafărul"** ("The Evening Star," 1883), a long Romantic narrative poem about the impossible love between a mortal and a celestial being, is the most cherished poem in the language. His shorter lyrics are studied by every Romanian schoolchild.

**Why it works:** Eminescu's command of rhyme, metre, and musicality is the deep end of the language, and reading him is how you come to feel Romanian as a literary instrument rather than a communication tool.

**What to watch for:** this is poetry, with all that implies -- inverted word order, archaic and poetic forms, compressed meaning, and vocabulary chosen for sound as much as sense. Even fluent readers lean on annotated editions. Approach Eminescu with a facing translation always at hand, read slowly, and accept that full appreciation is the work of years, not weeks. Start with a few short lyrics before attempting *Luceafărul*.

### Mircea Cărtărescu -- "Orbitor" and "Nostalgia"

**Mircea Cărtărescu** (born 1956) is the most celebrated living Romanian writer, widely held to be the finest prose stylist to emerge from post-communist Romania. His breakthrough **"Nostalgia"** (published in censored form in 1989, in full in 1993) is a set of interlinked novellas, and his trilogy **"Orbitor"** ("Blinding") is his vast, hallucinatory magnum opus.

**Why it works:** Cărtărescu writes contemporary Romanian at the highest level of ambition -- dreamlike, dense, image-saturated prose in the lineage of Kafka, Borges, and Pynchon. If you can read him, you can read anything.

**What to watch for:** *Orbitor* is hard -- genuinely, famously hard, with long baroque sentences, invented and rare vocabulary, and a deliberately disorienting structure. This is not where you build fluency; it is where you spend it. Begin with the more contained novellas of *Nostalgia* before approaching the trilogy, and even then expect to work. Save Cărtărescu for the moment when Romanian reading is already a pleasure and you want a summit to climb.

## How to Choose Your First Romanian Book

With the shelf laid out, here is how to actually pick -- because the right *first* book matters more than the right *best* book.

### Match the Book to Your Level, Not Your Ambition

The most common mistake is reaching straight for the classics because they are the names you have heard. Resist it. Creangă, Ispirescu, Rebreanu, and Eminescu are glorious, but their language is either archaic, regional, poetic, or all three, and starting there will exhaust you. Begin with the bilingual reader and *Micul Prinț* at A1-A2; move to Sebastian and Eliade's short fiction at B1-B2; keep the C1 names as goals to grow into. A book you finish at the right level teaches you more than a masterpiece you abandon on page thirty.

### Let the Cognates Carry You -- but Expect the Slavic Surprises

Lean into your Romance-language instincts. When you hit a long, Latinate-looking word, guess first -- you will usually be right, and that confidence keeps you reading. But stay alert for the high-frequency Slavic core -- *da*, *prieten*, *iubi*, *grijă*, *prost* -- and the everyday words that simply will not be guessable. These are not failures of the cognate strategy; they are the part of Romanian that makes it Romanian. Learning them in context, inside sentences you are already reading, is far more durable than drilling them in isolation.

### Use Parallel Text to Turn Any Book Into a Graded Reader

Because Romanian has so few purpose-built graded readers, the parallel-text method is not a luxury here -- it is the workaround that makes the whole literature available to you early. With an aligned English translation one tap away, you can attempt Sebastian or Eliade at a point where a monolingual edition would crush you, because every time you stall you can confirm the sentence and move on instead of grinding to a halt. Our [honest guide to parallel reading](/en/blog/parallel-reading-honest-guide/) covers when this method helps and its real limits -- worth reading before you build a habit around it.

## The Bottom Line

Romanian is one of the best-kept secrets in language learning for readers: a Category I Romance language where your Latin-rooted vocabulary opens half the page for free, attached to a literature -- from Creangă's fairy tales to Cărtărescu's hallucinations -- far larger and stranger than the country's size would suggest. The catches are real and worth respecting: the suffixed articles and surviving cases that no cognate prepares you for, the Slavic and substrate words that ambush you in everyday speech, and the beloved classics whose archaic, regional language makes them harder than their children's-book reputations imply.

The path through is the same one good readers take in every language, only more so here because the graded-reader shelf is thin. Start small and bilingual with a learner collection and *Micul Prinț*. Graduate to the clean modern prose of Sebastian and Eliade once the grammar feels automatic. Keep Rebreanu, Eminescu, and Cărtărescu as the peaks you are climbing toward, not the trail you start on. Let the cognates give you confidence and let parallel text and native audio carry you over the gaps the Slavic vocabulary opens up. Do that, and Romanian rewards reading-first learners as generously as any language on the map -- and a good deal more surprisingly.
