Dutch is one of the best-kept secrets in language learning. English speakers spend years agonising over which language is "worth" the effort, often gravitating toward the big, glamorous names, French, Spanish, Japanese, while overlooking the one Germanic language that is genuinely close to their own. Dutch sits right between English and German on the family tree, and the result is a language you can start reading sooner than almost any other. The Foreign Service Institute, which trains American diplomats, places Dutch in its easiest tier (Category I) alongside the Romance languages, requiring roughly 600 to 750 class hours to reach professional working proficiency. For reading specifically, where you control the pace and lean on the page rather than your ears, that head start is even more pronounced.

The reason is vocabulary. Open almost any Dutch text and you will see words you half-recognise before you have studied a single grammar rule. *Water* is *water*, *hand* is *hand*, *boek* is *book*, *huis* is *house*, *koffie* is *koffie*, *appel* is *apple*. Dutch shares its deepest Germanic roots with English, which means hundreds of everyday words are cognates or near-cognates, and the spelling, while it looks alien at first, maps onto sounds you already produce. This is the single biggest argument for learning Dutch by reading: the gap between "complete beginner" and "I can follow the gist of a real sentence" is unusually small.

That does not make Dutch trivial. Word order can twist in subordinate clauses, the definite articles *de* and *het* are assigned with no reliable logic, separable verbs split apart and scatter across the sentence, and the diminutive *-je* ending shows up everywhere. But none of these are reasons to delay. They are reasons to start with text rather than conversation, where you can see the structure laid out, reread it, and let the patterns settle in over hundreds of pages instead of being ambushed by them in real time.

This guide walks through real, verifiable Dutch books from absolute beginner to advanced literary fiction. For each one you will find the approximate CEFR level, why it works for learners, and what to watch out for. We will also cover how to choose your first Dutch book and how to use those cognates to your advantage.

## How to Choose Your First Dutch Book

The most common mistake is reaching too high. A motivated beginner buys a famous Dutch novel, opens it, understands four words in the first paragraph, and quietly shelves it forever. The fix is to choose a book where you can follow the *story* even when you cannot parse every word, because comprehension at the sentence level is what makes reading feel like progress rather than translation homework.

A practical rule: pick something where you recognise the meaning of most sentences after a little effort, not most words at a glance. At the very start, that almost always means graded readers or children's books written in short, concrete sentences. Save the literary novels for when you can read a page without your eyes constantly darting to a glossary. If you have never finished a book in another language before, our [guide to your first book in a foreign language](/en/blog/first-book-in-a-foreign-language/) walks through how to set the difficulty so you actually reach the last page.

### How Cognates Carry You Further Than You Expect

The English-Dutch overlap is large enough to change your reading strategy. When you hit an unfamiliar Dutch word, your first instinct should not be to reach for a dictionary, it should be to say the word aloud and listen for the English hiding inside it. *Drinken* is *drink*. *Slapen* is *sleep*. *Vergeten* is *forget*. *Schip* is *ship*. *Brood* is *bread*. Once you internalise a few spelling correspondences, Dutch *sch* often maps to English *sh* or *sc*, the Dutch *aa* and *oo* signal long vowels, *ij* and *ei* sound alike, you can decode a surprising amount without help.

This is exactly why reading is such an efficient way into Dutch, and why Dutch is a forgiving place to practise the parallel-reading method, where the translation sits one tap away. The cognates mean you spend less time stuck and more time reading, which is the only thing that actually builds vocabulary. If you are weighing Dutch against a Romance language, our comparison of [Romance versus Germanic languages for reading](/en/blog/romance-vs-germanic-languages-for-reading/) digs into where each family is easy and where it bites.

## Beginner Dutch Books (A1-A2)

At the beginner stage your goal is volume and momentum, not literary merit. You want sentences short enough to hold in your head, vocabulary that repeats often enough to stick, and a story interesting enough to pull you to the next page. The books below are built for exactly that.

### Short Stories in Dutch for Beginners, Olly Richards

This is the standard recommendation for a reason. Olly Richards' *Short Stories in Dutch for Beginners* (Teach Yourself, 2019) is a collection of eight original stories written specifically for learners and mapped to the A2-B1 range of the CEFR. The stories span genres, science fiction, crime, history, thriller, which keeps the reading from feeling like a textbook exercise, and the vocabulary is deliberately controlled and recycled so that words you meet in chapter one return often enough to lodge in memory.

**Level:** A2 (workable from a strong A1).

**Why it works:** Each chapter comes with a bilingual word list for the words highlighted in the text, a plain-language plot summary in Dutch, and comprehension questions, so you can read without constantly stopping and check that you followed the plot. The chapters are short, which makes finishing one a realistic daily goal, and finishing things is what keeps beginners going.

**What to watch for:** It is a learning tool, not literature, and the prose can feel a little flat. That is the right trade-off at this level, but do not expect to be moved. Treat it as a confidence-builder and a vocabulary engine, then move on.

### Jip en Janneke, Annie M.G. Schmidt

If there is one piece of native Dutch writing every learner should know about, it is Annie M.G. Schmidt's *Jip en Janneke*. Schmidt (1911-1995) is the undisputed queen of Dutch children's literature, she won the Hans Christian Andersen Medal in 1988, and these little stories about two children who live next door to each other have sold over five million copies since the 1950s. They were originally written as a weekly newspaper feuilleton, which is why each one is short, self-contained, and built around a single small adventure.

**Level:** A2 (genuinely accessible, with patience, from late A1).

**Why it works:** The language is simple, concrete, and full of the everyday vocabulary you actually need, eating, playing, getting dirty, going to bed. Sentences are short. The situations are universal enough that you can often predict the meaning, which lets you absorb how Dutch sentences are built rather than decoding them word by word. For many learners this is the first native text they read end to end, and that milestone matters.

**What to watch for:** It is written for small children, so the content is gentle and occasionally repetitive, and a few expressions are charmingly old-fashioned. None of that hurts you as a learner, repetition is exactly what you want, but go in knowing this is sweet rather than gripping.

### Pluk van de Petteflet, Annie M.G. Schmidt

Schmidt's second classic, *Pluk van de Petteflet* (1971), is a step up from *Jip en Janneke* in length and ambition. Pluk is a small boy who drives a little red tow truck and gets caught up in a series of connected adventures, illustrated, as so much of Schmidt's work was, by Fiep Westendorp. It has remained continuously in print for over half a century and is one of the most beloved children's books in the Netherlands.

**Level:** A2-B1.

**Why it works:** Because it is a continuous story rather than disconnected vignettes, *Pluk* rewards you for building momentum, and the recurring characters mean the vocabulary keeps cycling back. The prose is still very readable but stretches you a little further than *Jip en Janneke*, making it a natural next book once those shorter stories feel comfortable. Schmidt's famously playful, idiomatic Dutch also starts to show here, giving you a gentle first taste of how the language sounds when a real stylist is at work.

**What to watch for:** A little more vocabulary and slightly longer sentences mean you will lean on a glossary more often than with the *Jip en Janneke* stories. That is the point, it is a bridge book, but choose it as a second or third read, not your very first.

### De kleine prins (The Little Prince), Antoine de Saint-Exupéry

Saint-Exupéry's *Le Petit Prince* is the perennial language-learner's friend, and the Dutch translation, *De kleine prins*, is no exception. The first Dutch translation appeared in 1951, and the book has since been published in countless editions, including bilingual English-Dutch parallel versions. Because the original is a deceptively simple fable, the Dutch reads cleanly without dumbing anything down.

**Level:** A2-B1.

**Why it works:** Many learners already know the story, which is an enormous advantage, when you can predict what happens next, you can devote your attention to *how* it is said in Dutch rather than puzzling out the plot. The vocabulary is concrete (roses, foxes, stars, kings) and the sentences are short and declarative. The bilingual editions make it an easy entry point into parallel reading, where the English sits beside the Dutch and you only glance over when you genuinely need to.

**What to watch for:** It is a translation, so it teaches you correct, natural Dutch but not the particular rhythms of a Dutch-born writer. It also carries a melancholy, philosophical undertone in places that can run slightly ahead of A2 vocabulary. Pair it with a parallel translation and you will be fine, our [honest guide to parallel reading](/en/blog/parallel-reading-honest-guide/) explains how to use the facing text without becoming dependent on it.

A note on graded readers: the Dutch-as-a-second-language (NT2) world also produces a steady stream of *makkelijk lezen* ("easy reading") titles, short, level-controlled books written specifically for adult learners, often tied to the NT2 examination levels. They vary in availability and are usually bought through Dutch-language bookshops rather than international retailers, but if you can get hold of them they are excellent A1-A2 material, written for grown-ups rather than children. If you would rather see how levels map across many languages at once, our [best books by language level](/en/blog/best-books-by-language-level/) breakdown puts Dutch in that wider context.

## Intermediate Dutch Books (B1-B2)

This is the stage where reading becomes genuinely rewarding, where you stop reading *to learn Dutch* and start reading *in Dutch* because the book is good. You can handle real sentences now, you recognise most common vocabulary, and you can tolerate the occasional unknown word without losing the thread. The books below are real books written for native adults, chosen because they remain accessible to a determined intermediate reader.

### Het Achterhuis (The Diary of a Young Girl), Anne Frank

Anne Frank's diary, *Het Achterhuis*, is one of the most important books ever written in Dutch, and, almost by accident, one of the best intermediate reading choices a learner can make. First published by her father Otto in 1947, it records the two years the family spent in hiding in Amsterdam, written in the voice of a teenager. That voice is the key: Anne writes in clear, direct, often conversational Dutch, the language of a bright fifteen-year-old rather than a seasoned novelist.

**Level:** B1-B2.

**Why it works:** Because it is a diary, the entries are short and self-contained, so you can read in small, complete units and stop whenever you need to. The vocabulary is the language of daily life, family, food, fear, boredom, hope, which is exactly the vocabulary you most want to own. And the emotional pull is real; this is a book you will want to keep reading, which is the single most important quality any learning text can have. The Dutch original entered the public domain in many countries in 2016, so editions are widely available.

**What to watch for:** The subject matter is heavy, and the historical and wartime context introduces vocabulary you will not meet elsewhere. Some passages reflect 1940s Dutch usage. None of this is a barrier at B1-B2, but it is an emotionally demanding read, not a light one, approach it with that in mind.

### Het Diner (The Dinner), Herman Koch

Herman Koch's *Het Diner* (2009) is the modern Dutch novel that broke through internationally, it sold over a million copies in Europe, won the NS Publieksprijs, and has been translated into more than twenty languages. Two couples meet for an expensive dinner in an Amsterdam restaurant, and across the courses of the meal a dark secret about their teenage sons is slowly revealed. The structure, aperitif, starter, main course, dessert, gives the book a tight, propulsive shape.

**Level:** B2.

**Why it works:** Koch writes in clean, contemporary, conversational Dutch, short sentences, lots of dialogue, a first-person narrator who talks directly to you. There is very little ornate vocabulary or literary flourish to slow you down. The tension is what keeps you reading, and because the setting is so ordinary (a meal, a conversation), the vocabulary is modern and useful rather than archaic or specialised. For many learners this is the first "grown-up" novel they finish in Dutch.

**What to watch for:** The narrator is unreliable and increasingly unsettling, and the book's pleasure depends on picking up on irony and what is left unsaid, which is harder in a second language. You may finish a chapter sure you understood every word and still miss the menace underneath. That is fine; reread the key scenes. It is also genuinely dark in its themes, so know what you are getting into.

If you are reading Dutch and German together, a very natural pairing, given how close the two languages are, you may find it useful to alternate between this guide and its [German counterpart](/en/blog/best-books-to-learn-german-through-reading/), since many learners pick up both Germanic languages in tandem.

### Oeroeg, Hella S. Haasse

Hella S. Haasse (1918-2011), often called the grand dame of Dutch letters, made her debut in 1948 with the novella *Oeroeg*, and it remains a staple of Dutch literary education, the kind of book Dutch schoolchildren are assigned. The anonymous narrator grows up on a plantation in the Dutch East Indies (colonial Indonesia) and forms a deep childhood friendship with Oeroeg, a local boy; as they grow up, the political and racial realities of colonial life slowly pull them apart.

**Level:** B2.

**Why it works:** *Oeroeg* is short, a novella rather than a novel, which makes it a realistic literary target for an upper-intermediate reader. Haasse's prose is precise and controlled rather than flamboyant, so although it is "real literature," the sentences are easier to follow than the reputation might suggest. It also opens a window onto an important and uncomfortable chapter of Dutch history that you will not get from contemporary fiction.

**What to watch for:** The colonial setting brings vocabulary and place names that will not generalise to everyday Dutch, and the emotional and political subtext is more demanding than the sentence-level language. It is short, but it is not breezy. Read it when you can already handle a full novel and want something with literary weight.

## Advanced Dutch Books (C1+)

At the advanced stage you are reading for the writing itself, the style, the ambiguity, the ideas, and you can handle long sentences, shifting registers, and vocabulary you have to infer from context. The books below are the genuine article: major works of Dutch literature that reward the effort it takes to read them. Expect to slow down, and expect that to be worth it.

### De Aanslag (The Assault), Harry Mulisch

Harry Mulisch is one of the towering figures of postwar Dutch literature, and *De Aanslag* (1982) is the novel that cemented his reputation at home and abroad. It opens with a single violent night in Haarlem in the final winter of the German occupation, a collaborator is shot, and the Nazis retaliate against the wrong family, and then follows the lone survivor across the next thirty-five years in five episodes, dated 1945, 1952, 1956, 1966, and 1981. The film adaptation won the 1986 Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film.

**Level:** C1+.

**Why it works:** Mulisch writes with great clarity for a literary author, the structure is clean and the plot genuinely gripping, which carries you through the more demanding passages. Because the story unfolds across decades, you watch the same vocabulary and themes return in new contexts, deepening as you go. As an entry point into serious Dutch literature it is unusually approachable: it reads, at one level, like a superior thriller.

**What to watch for:** Beneath the thriller surface, the novel is a meditation on guilt, memory, and the moral ambiguity of occupation, and much of its meaning is carried by implication. The wartime and historical vocabulary is specialised. This is a C1 read not because the sentences are impenetrable but because the *point* of the book lives between the lines.

### Het volgende verhaal / Rituelen, Cees Nooteboom

Cees Nooteboom (1933-2026) is the Dutch writer most often described as a European author par excellence, philosophical, cosmopolitan, preoccupied with time, memory, and identity. Two of his novels make natural advanced reading. *Rituelen* (1980) is the book that made his name in the Netherlands and the first to be translated into English; *Het volgende verhaal* (1991), written for the Dutch Boekenweek and winner of the Aristeion Prize, is short, strange, and luminous, a man falls asleep in Amsterdam and wakes in a Lisbon hotel room he stayed in years before.

**Level:** C1+.

**Why it works:** Nooteboom's prose is some of the finest in modern Dutch, and reading it is the closest thing to hearing the language sing. *Het volgende verhaal* in particular is short, which makes it a manageable C1 target despite its density. If you want to know what Dutch is capable of as a literary instrument, this is where to look.

**What to watch for:** Nooteboom is allusive and abstract; the difficulty is conceptual as much as linguistic. Long, layered sentences, classical and literary references, and a deliberately disorienting structure mean you will reread paragraphs. Come here only when reading Dutch is already comfortable and you are chasing depth rather than plot.

A broader point about the advanced shelf: the so-called "great" Dutch writers, Mulisch, Nooteboom, Haasse, alongside Willem Frederik Hermans and Gerard Reve, are where the language's full range lives, but they are also where the [intermediate plateau](/en/blog/intermediate-plateau-how-to-break-through/) is most often broken. Reading one demanding novel slowly and completely will do more for your Dutch than skimming five easy ones.

## The Bottom Line

Dutch rewards readers faster than almost any language an English speaker can choose. The vocabulary overlap is real, the grammar is approachable once you can see it laid out on the page, and the FSI's easiest-tier ranking is not an exaggeration, it reflects a genuine head start. The trick is to honour your actual level. Start with Olly Richards' short stories and Annie M.G. Schmidt's *Jip en Janneke* and *Pluk van de Petteflet*, where short sentences and everyday words let you build momentum. Move up to Anne Frank's *Het Achterhuis* and Herman Koch's *Het Diner* once you can follow real adult prose. Save Mulisch and Nooteboom for when you are reading for the writing itself.

Read books slightly below where your ego wants to be, finish them, and let the cognates do their quiet work. Lean on parallel translations and native audio so a hard sentence becomes a pause rather than a full stop. Do that consistently and you will be reading genuine Dutch literature sooner than you think, because with Dutch, the distance from your first page to your first novel is shorter than the difficulty rankings let on.
